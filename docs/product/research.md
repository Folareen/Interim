# Interim: Research Brief

Owns the evidence behind the shift adopted in overview.md version 1.2: Interim built first for people whose body changes because of serious illness, with a supportive voice as the core and fashion and beauty following from it. The rules themselves stay in [overview.md](overview.md). Nothing here is product copy.

Searched October 20, 2026. Most figures were read from abstracts, press summaries or search snippets, not full papers. Check each one against the original and record the date before it appears in any public copy.

## 1. Summary

* Appearance change from serious illness is common, distressing and under-served. It drives people to hide, delay going back to work and pull back from social life. In a few reports, people have weighed turning down care because of hair loss.
* Clinicians and charities agree it matters, but support is scattered: one-off workshops, wig shops, condition-specific forums. Nothing follows a person across the whole timeline of change.
* The support that has evidence is not cheerleading. It is practical help with looking like yourself, self-compassion, and skills for handling other people's reactions. Forced positivity and battle language can make some people feel worse.
* Interim's stage model already fits this. The change, now adopted, makes the companion voice and the everyday moments the core of each check in, and treats outfits, hair and makeup as tools for those moments.
* The main risks are sliding into therapy claims, regulated in some US states since 2025, and losing the hackathon's differentiation from Bloom. Both can be handled (section 7).

## 2. The problem, with evidence

**Hair loss.**
* A 2023 review in Current Oncology puts chemotherapy hair loss at about 65% of people who receive it (already cited in [overview.md](overview.md)).
* Hair loss is linked to anxiety, depression, poorer body image and lower self-esteem. People with high distress about it are reported as 1.5 times more likely to be depressed ([narrative review, Annals of Palliative Medicine](https://apm.amegroups.org/article/view/127199/html)).
* Women with breast cancer rank it third among the worst side effects, after nausea and vomiting. In a small Maltese study, most ranked it first ([University of Malta](https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/handle/123456789/65513)).
* A 2026 JMIR Cancer study on scalp cooling concludes that its value is mostly psychological, not the hair kept ([JMIR Cancer](https://cancer.jmir.org/2026/1/e94635)).
* Outside cancer, alopecia areata carries more anxiety and depression than controls. A 2023 meta-analysis covered 6,398 people. A 2025 study of 596 people found 81% with anxiety or depressive symptoms, and stigma predicted distress better than how severe the hair loss was. That study had industry funding ([HCPLive](https://hcplive.com/view/alopecia-versus-vitiligo-spotlighting-differences-in-psychological-impact), [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/skin-health/alopecia-areata-associated-with-severe-psychosocial-impact)).

**Body image after illness.**
* Body image distress is estimated at 31% to 67% of US breast cancer survivors (secondary citation of ACS) and up to 74% in head and neck cancer ([JMIR protocol, PMC12527319](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12527319/)).
* Studies use many different scales (19 across 25 studies in one review), so no single pooled figure exists ([Springer review](https://www.springermedizin.de/body-image-in-cancer-survivors-a-systematic-review-of-case-contr/9053170)).
* Long-term steroids cause moon face in up to 63% of people in one study. In an ITP survey, 43 of 63 respondents said face swelling bothered them "a lot" ([Wikipedia summary of the literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon_face)). The change often fades after the dose comes down, so it fits the stage model, which expects change to reverse.
* Vitiligo: a review of 168 studies reports depression in up to 62% and anxiety in up to 68% of samples, with wide ranges ([Springer](https://www.springermedicine.com/mood-disorders/affective-disorder/psychosocial-effects-of-vitiligo-a-systematic-literature-review/22248800)).

**Everyday life, not just the mirror.**
* Korean breast cancer survivors returning to work most often wanted to hide their changed appearance. They felt at ease when colleagues treated them as before ([e-mch](https://www.e-mch.org/journal/view.php?number=201)).
* After head and neck cancer, appearance change made some people delay work, change jobs or email instead of meeting colleagues ([NHS HRA summary](https://www.hra.nhs.uk/planning-and-improving-research/application-summaries/research-summaries/perspectives-on-returning-to-work-after-head-and-neck-cancer/)).
* People with a stoma who could not dress the way they wanted took part less in social life. Their clothing adjustments were trial and error and personal ([PMC3536890](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3536890), [Colostomy UK](https://colostomyuk.org/?p=4735)).
* After mastectomy, bras and clothing shape a sense of "normalcy and control", and ready-to-wear rarely fits ([PMC11100881](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11100881/), [PMC7321884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7321884)).
* UK charity Changing Faces surveys find 47% of people with a visible difference feel self-conscious or embarrassed, and a third have faced a hate crime ([Changing Faces](https://www.changingfaces.org.uk/news/new-research-worrying-trend-hate/)).

**Scale.**
* 18.6 million cancer survivors in the US on January 1, 2025, projected above 22 million by 2035. Breast cancer has the largest projected growth, to about 5.3 million ([ACS via ASCO Post](https://ascopost.com/issues/june-25-2025/number-of-cancer-survivors-in-the-united-states-reaches-186-million-and-projected-to-exceed-22-million-by-2035)).
* Adaptive clothing estimates disagree by orders of magnitude depending on definition. A semi-medical segment that includes post-surgery wear is valued at $7.8B (2023) by Allied Market Research. Treat all of these as soft ([Allied](https://www.alliedmarketresearch.com/press-release/semi-medical-adaptive-clothing-market.html)).

## 3. What actually helps

These findings are the design brief for the companion voice.

| Approach | Evidence | What Interim takes from it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Practical appearance help** (Look Good Feel Better) | A non-randomised study of 121 women found better mood and felt attractiveness after one class. Self-reported confidence went from 50% to 92% across 26,000 participants in 16 countries, but that survey was run by the program itself ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00520-009-0620-8), [LGFB](https://lgfb.org.au/look-good-feel-better-global-survey-shows-positive-impact-on-women-undergoing-cancer-treatment/)) | Concrete, doable steps help. This is what Stage Cards already do. |
| **Self-compassion writing** (My Changed Body) | RCT, 304 women with breast cancer, JCO 2018. One 30-minute online exercise reduced body image distress and raised body appreciation, with effects at 1 and 3 months. Compliance was 88% ([Macquarie](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/reducing-body-image-related-distress-in-women-with-breast-cancer-/)). A pilot found similar results for visible skin conditions ([PMC8114345](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8114345)) | Brief, kind, self-directed reflection works and fits a check in. It must be offered, never forced. |
| **Social skills for reactions** (Face IT) | RCT, 83 adults with a visible difference. The 8-session online CBT and social-skills course reduced anxiety, depression and appearance concern, with gains held at 6 months ([UWE](https://uwe-repository.worktribe.com/output/951590/evaluation-of-the-effectiveness-of-face-it-a-computer-based-psychosocial-intervention-for-disfigurement-related-distress)) | Help people prepare for moments: what to say when someone stares or asks, how to walk into the office the first day. |
| **Being treated as before** | Return-to-work study above | Keep the person's own style at the centre. "Looking like yourself" is the right promise. |
| **Empathetic AI chat** | Pilot, 5 young people with cancer, GPT-4, no control group. 4 of 5 reported less anxiety. About 10-minute sessions every 2 to 3 days ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2025.1543543/full)) | Weak but encouraging. The cadence matches Interim's check ins. |

## 4. What harms

* **Forced positivity.** In a Peter MacCallum study (in the journal Cancer), optimism did not predict survival. The authors questioned encouraging optimism when it leads people to hide distress ([NBC](https://www.nbcnews.com/health/health-news/hope-not-seen-helping-cancer-patients-flna1C9445465)). Survivor writing describes "stay positive" as guilt-inducing ([Cancer Today](https://www.cancertodaymag.org/winter-2025-2026/the-downside-of-too-much-upside/)).
* **Battle language.** In experiments with about 1,000 healthy readers, war metaphors made cancer seem harder to deal with and more fatalistic (Hauser and Schwarz). Semino's corpus of 1.5 million words found that some people find fighting language motivating and others feel blamed by it, worst near the end of life. Her recommendation is a menu of framings the person chooses from ([Lancaster](https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/news/articles/2014/battle-metaphors-for-cancer-can-be-harmful/), [Time](https://time.com/3636102/fighting-cancer)).
* **Agreeable chatbots.** Companion bots have been named in lawsuits after users in crisis were harmed. Critics point to their habit of telling people what they want to hear ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/local/chicago/2025/08/06/illinois-ai-therapy-ban-mental-health-regulation)).
* **Before-and-after framing.** This is already covered by the no-goal, no-streak and no-future-body rules in [overview.md](overview.md).

## 5. Existing solutions and the gap

| Category | Examples | What they miss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In-person appearance workshops | Look Good Feel Better | One session, location-bound, mostly makeup. No follow-up as the person keeps changing. |
| Wig and headwear try-on | Perfect Corp generative wig try-on (NAO-ART, Japan), TryHair on Shopify. Bloom in this hackathon | One shopping moment. Nothing for regrowth, weight or fit change, or the person's everyday plans ([Perfect Corp](https://www.perfectcorp.com/business/news/nao-art-partnership-perfect-corp), [Shopify](https://apps.shopify.com/tryhair-wig-tryon)). |
| Cancer navigation apps | Outcomes4Me (over 400,000 users, $38M raised), Belong.Life | Medical navigation: care options, trials. Appearance and daily confidence are out of scope ([Outcomes4Me](https://outcomes4me.com/press-release/outcomes4me-raises-4-7-million-to-help-patients-navigate-cancer-care-using-ai/)). |
| Psychological programs | Face IT, My Changed Body, Changing Faces support | Strong evidence, but research tools or charity services that are hard to find. No link to what to wear tomorrow. |
| Adaptive and post-surgery clothing | Specialist brands for mastectomy and ostomy wear | Products, not guidance. Fit is trial and error. |
| General AI companions | Chat apps | Not grounded in the person's real photos or stage. Agreeable by design. Increasingly regulated. |

**The gap:** no product combines (a) a measured record of how someone's appearance is changing, (b) practical help looking like themselves at each stage, and (c) a steady, honest, kind voice that helps them get through the moments that matter: work, a wedding, a date, a video call, a first trip outside. Interim already has (a) and most of (b).

## 6. What was adopted

Adopted on October 20, 2026. The rules now live in [overview.md](overview.md#the-companion) (companion, tone, moments, words for today, distress handoff, language rules) and [spec.md](spec.md) (enforcement). This brief keeps only the evidence. How each finding maps:

* Practical help (Look Good Feel Better): the Stage Card stays the core, now planned for a moment.
* Self-compassion (My Changed Body): the optional on-device reflection prompt.
* Social skills (Face IT, Changing Faces): moments and the what to say lines.
* The metaphor menu (Semino): the person picks a tone, including *Just practical*.
* Forced positivity and battle language: the companion phrase scan.
* Agreeable chatbots and the state laws: not therapy, distress handoff in plain code, reflection never reaches the model.
* Faith and comfort: words for today, opt-in, from fixed public domain texts, never written by the model.

## 7. Risks and tensions

* **Regulation.** Illinois (WOPR Act, 2025, $10,000 fines) and Nevada ban AI from giving mental health care. Utah requires disclosure ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/local/chicago/2025/08/06/illinois-ai-therapy-ban-mental-health-regulation)). Interim must stay a cosmetic and practical tool with a kind voice, never therapy. The FDA's January 2026 general wellness guidance keeps low-risk wellness tools outside device rules if they make no diagnosis or treatment claims ([Foley](https://www.foley.com/p/102meea/digital-health-policy-fda-relaxes-restrictions-over-wearables-and-ai-decision-ma/)). This fits the existing banned-word rules. It is not legal advice, and a real buyer would need counsel.
* **Bloom.** Bloom is built around chemo wig try-on, so leading with serious illness puts Interim closer to it. The difference is stated in [overview.md](overview.md#templates): the whole timeline, several situations, everyday moments and the companion voice, not a single try-on.
* **Hackathon judging.** The GLP 1 numbers are the strongest market evidence. Illness-first is a stronger human story but has softer numbers, so the demo leads with the hair loss timeline and keeps weight change as proof the engine generalises ([overview.md](overview.md#demo-plan)).
* **Demo ethics.** The demo needs dated photos of a consenting person who has been through serious illness. That is harder to get than GLP 1 or beard photos. Sample profiles must be labelled.
* **The model's voice.** The companion note is model output, so it gets the banned-word scan, the companion phrase scan ([spec.md](spec.md) section 13) and fixture tests on hard cases.
* **Over-reach.** "Live well every day" can drift into fitness, food and medical territory. Interim should stay with how you look, what you wear and how you walk into a room.

## 8. Answers to the open questions

Decided October 20, 2026, to be checked in the interviews ([spec.md](spec.md) section 18).

* **Which tone do people pick?** Ask once after the situation, with *Warm* preselected, because most people starting in a sensitive situation are looking for some warmth and the choice is one tap to change on every card. *Just practical* is always visible beside it. The interviews test whether the preselection should go.
* **Note first or last?** First, at most three sentences, so the person is met before the product. In *Just practical* it is only the change and the step, so it never gets in the way. The interviews show both orders.
* **Which regions for the distress handoff?** US and Canada (988), UK and Ireland (Samaritans, 116 123), Australia (Lifeline, 13 11 14), and findahelpline.com everywhere else. The region comes from the browser locale and can be changed. Each number is checked against the official service before launch.
* **Charity or clinic as first buyer?** No. Charities and cancer centres are distribution partners, free for the people they support. Revenue stays with telehealth and supportive care programs, retailers (adding adaptive and post-surgery brands) and headwear and haircare brands ([overview.md](overview.md#who-pays)).
* **Daily quotes and verses?** Yes, opt-in. Sets at launch: Gentle words, Christian (KJV), Muslim (Pickthall, 1930) and Jewish (JPS 1917), all public domain. Passages that promise a physical outcome, use banned or battle words, or say "be strong" are left out. For example, Psalm 147:3 ("healeth"), Romans 12:12 ("patient") and Psalm 27:14 ("Be strong") are not used. Hindu and Buddhist sets wait for passages checked by someone from each tradition. The public domain passages checked so far (Arnold's Gita, Müller's Dhammapada) lean on death or on thoughts causing suffering, which reads badly for this audience. Before public launch, someone from each tradition reviews that tradition's set.
